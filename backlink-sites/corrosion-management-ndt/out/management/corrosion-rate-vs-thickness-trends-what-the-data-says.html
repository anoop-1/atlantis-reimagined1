Corrosion Rate vs Thickness Trends: What the Long-Term Data Actually Tells You

By Hannah O\'Connell, NACE/AMPP CIP III · Published 2026-05-10

This article walks through the practical decisions corrosion engineers, AMPP-certified inspectors, materials managers need to make when working on corrosion management & monitoring. The goal is not theory — it is the kind of working knowledge an experienced inspector or engineer would share over a coffee on a job site, with enough rigor to point back to code and standard references when those decisions get challenged in audit. Throughout the piece we focus on the parts of corrosion rate that turn into real schedule or budget pain when they go wrong.

Why this comes up so often in the field

Ask any corrosion engineers how often corrosion rate drives schedule pain and you will hear the same answer: more often than the project plan ever assumes. Part of the reason is that this kind of inspection sits at the intersection of multiple owners — operations, integrity, fabrication, and QA — and the ownership boundaries are rarely clean. When a problem surfaces, it is usually too late to plan; the team is already in execution mode and looking for guidance.

The framing in this guide assumes you are the person who has to make a defensible call quickly, document it well, and live with the consequences. Where standards apply, we cite the specific clauses; where the standards leave a gap, we describe how leading operators close that gap in practice.

Scope of this guide

We cover four things explicitly:

  • The decision points that drive most corrosion rate programs disputes between QA, integrity, and operations.
  • The minimum data a defensible decision needs — what to record, in what format, and where it lives in the long term.
  • Common failure modes we see when teams skip steps under schedule pressure.
  • Reference standards that translate between geographies (US, EU, Middle East, Asia-Pacific).

Technique selection in practice

The textbook approach to corrosion rate usually points engineers at one or two techniques. The field reality is messier; the technique that ranks best on a comparison chart is often not the one that fits the access, the schedule, or the inspector skill mix actually available on the day. We walk through three patterns we see repeatedly.

Pattern A: prove the absence of a known mechanism

When the integrity model already predicts a specific damage mechanism — say HTHA in a hydroprocessing reactor or SCC in a chloride-bearing line — the inspection design needs to be written around finding that mechanism, not around generic surface coverage. Probability of detection (POD) becomes the design variable. Pattern B: find what you are not expecting

Screening campaigns are different. Their job is to flag anomalies you did not predict. POD is still important, but coverage and false-call rate matter more, because the field team has finite time to chase any flag the screening throws up. Pattern C: requalify after a repair

Post-repair inspection is the most procedural of the three patterns and the most likely to be weakened by schedule pressure. The required NDT is usually defined by the original construction code (ASME VIII, ASME XI, AWS D1.1, and so on), which is fine — but the decision about how to apply it under field conditions is rarely covered. The data the audit will ask for

Auditors do not argue with conclusions; they argue with the evidence behind them. For any defensible corrosion rate report, the evidence packet needs to include at minimum:

  • The qualified procedure (revision, approval signatures, applicable code edition).
  • The personnel certification records (level, method, and currency).
  • The calibration records for the day of inspection (block IDs, calibration block traceability).
  • The raw data set, archived in a format that can be re-evaluated by an independent reviewer years later.
  • The decision rationale — written, dated, and signed by the responsible Level III or PE.

The last bullet is where most programs fail an audit. It does not take much: a one-paragraph note attached to the report explaining why the team chose method X with parameters Y is usually enough. Without it, the report stands alone and any later question becomes a guessing game.

Common pitfalls (and how to avoid them)

  • Reusing a procedure without a delta-review. The procedure was written for an original scope; the new scope has different geometry, different access, or different acceptance criteria. Even one of those differences can invalidate the procedure for the new application.
  • Skipping the calibration block check. A worn or unverified calibration block is the single most common reason high-quality equipment generates poor data.
  • Letting one inspector own the data interpretation alone. Interpretation of corrosion rate should always have a second reviewer for any indication that drives a fitness or repair decision.
  • Treating the report as the deliverable. The deliverable is the decision the report enables. A well-formatted report that does not let the asset owner act is a failure.

Frequently asked questions

How long does this work typically add to a turnaround schedule?

It depends on how much of the work was pre-planned. Pre-planned screening can usually fit inside the existing critical path; reactive corrosion rate after an unexpected finding can extend a turnaround by 24–96 hours per equipment item, sometimes more. The cheapest hour you will ever buy is the planning hour you spent six months earlier.

Who signs the decision?

Best practice is a Level III for the NDT decision and a fixed-equipment or integrity engineer (PE/CEng) for the fitness decision. The two are different decisions even when they share the same data.

What changes when the asset is in a regulated environment?

The data you keep, how long you keep it, and who can review it. The technical work usually does not change much — the documentation effort goes up significantly.

Closing thoughts

If we had to summarize corrosion rate in one line it would be this: the technique matters less than the decision discipline around it. Teams that consistently choose the right technique are usually teams that have invested in writing down their decision rationale, qualifying their procedures with care, and keeping their inspectors current. Equipment and software change every few years; that discipline does not.

That framing usually changes the procurement conversation, the training conversation, and the audit conversation in the same direction.

If your team is being asked to justify investment in corrosion rate, the easiest place to start is a one-page side-by-side: current state, gap, expected uplift, and the specific risk ranking that improves. The numbers do not have to be precise; they have to be defensible.

Operator behaviours that actually move the needle on thickness trend

Three behaviours we see at high-performing operators consistently show up in thickness trend programs that are working: written rationale for every off-procedure decision; a standing peer-review for indications that drive a fitness call; and a quarterly close-out review where the integrity engineer and the inspection lead sit down with the raw data, not just the summary. None of these behaviours require new hardware. All three improve outcomes more than another tool purchase usually does.

What we do not see at high-performing operators is over-reliance on a single inspector or a single contractor. Cross-checking — even informally — is the cheapest insurance you can buy on a thickness trend program.

Documentation patterns worth borrowing

The best CML trending documentation packages we have seen share three patterns:

  • A short narrative section at the front of every report explaining the inspection objective in plain English. Auditors, regulators, and new hires all read the narrative first.
  • A consistent indication-numbering scheme that survives across multiple inspection campaigns, so an indication found in 2024 can be tracked through 2026 and 2028 without renaming.
  • An attached "open items" list with target dates, ownership, and the trigger that closes each item. This is the single most useful artifact for a turnaround manager.

If your current report template is missing one of those, that is the easiest documentation improvement you can make this quarter, and it costs nothing beyond a template update.

Procurement and contract considerations

corrosion data analysis engagements have a recurring procurement pitfall: the scope of work is written in inspection-deliverable language ("ten welds inspected by PAUT"), and the contract success criteria are written in commercial language ("on time, on budget, no NCRs"). Neither captures the engineering outcome the asset owner actually needs, which is a defensible decision about fitness for service. Contracts that explicitly include a deliverable like "decision-grade report with Level III rationale signed and dated" align all three parties — owner, integrity engineer, and contractor — around the same outcome.

Training and competency considerations

Tooling improvements in corrosion rate usually outpace inspector training by 18–24 months. Phased array systems, advanced eddy current arrays, and automated scanners are now common; structured training pathways for those tools are less common. The implication for hiring managers is straightforward: budget for technique-specific training as part of the equipment purchase, not as a separate line item to be defended later.

Competency on a written exam does not equal competency on a job site. The most reliable way to check applied competency is a witnessed scan of a known-flaw block, evaluated independently by a Level III who did not run the scan.
