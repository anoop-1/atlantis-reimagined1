The ASNT NDT Level III magnetic particle testing (MT) exam is a 90-question, 2-hour computer-based method exam. You need 70% to pass. Combined with the Level III Basic, it earns an ASNT NDT Level III certificate in MT. It tests the magnetism theory behind every shot: ferromagnetism and the hysteresis loop, how current type and technique decide field direction and depth, how field adequacy is verified, how particles, carriers and lighting affect sensitivity, demagnetisation, interpretation, and the codes and procedures an MT Level III writes and approves. MT is one of the shorter Level III method exams, but its theory is deeper than most Level IIs expect.

Who MT Level III exam prep is for

MT is very often a second or third Level III method. A candidate who has passed the Basic and a UT or RT method exam adds MT and PT because an employer's written practice needs a Level III for every method the company performs, and surface methods are performed everywhere: weld inspection in fabrication shops, in-service crack checks on refinery equipment, forgings and castings in manufacturing, and components in aerospace and rail.

Candidates also come from wet horizontal bench work in manufacturing, where they have run coil shots and head shots thousands of times but rarely had to explain why a given amperage was chosen. Others come from field yoke work on welds and have little bench experience at all. Both need to cover the side they lack. The exam treats bench, portable, stationary and specialised techniques as equally fair game.

What the MT Level III exam covers

We organise the ASNT MT body of knowledge, which follows the ANSI/ASNT CP-105 magnetic particle topical outline at Level III depth, into six domains.

1. Principles of magnetism

Ferromagnetic, paramagnetic and diamagnetic materials, magnetic domains, flux density (B) and magnetising force (H), permeability and reluctance, the hysteresis loop with retentivity, residual magnetism, coercive force and saturation, and leakage fields at discontinuities. Expect questions that read a hysteresis curve and ask what a material will do, such as why a high-retentivity steel suits the residual method and a low-carbon steel does not.

2. Magnetising currents and their effects

Alternating current, half-wave rectified and full-wave rectified current, and direct current. How each behaves at depth (skin effect), why AC gives particle mobility and good surface sensitivity, why HWDC is favoured for near-surface detection with dry powder, and how current type affects demagnetisation. This is one of the most heavily tested areas.

3. Techniques and field direction

Longitudinal magnetisation with coils (fill factor, L/D ratio), circular magnetisation with head shots, central conductors and prods, yokes, multidirectional equipment, continuous versus residual methods, and the rule that a discontinuity shows best when it lies across the field. A Level III must design coverage: which shots, in which directions, at what current, to find discontinuities of any orientation on a given part.

4. Field adequacy and verification

Hall-effect meters and tangential field measurement, quantitative quality indicators and shims, pie gauges and their limits, and the amperage rules of thumb found in the governing standards. Know what each indicator actually proves, and what it does not.

5. Particles, carriers, lighting and demagnetisation

Dry versus wet, visible versus fluorescent, particle size and shape, carrier fluids, concentration checks by settling test, contamination, UV-A intensity and ambient visible light limits for fluorescent inspection, and white-light requirements for visible inspection. Also demagnetisation: why and when it is required, reversing-and-decreasing methods, and how residual field is checked.

6. Interpretation, codes and procedures

Relevant, non-relevant and false indications, discontinuities by product form and process, and how acceptance criteria are applied. Procedure content, the role of ASTM E1444 and E709, ASME Section V Article 7, and AWS D1.1 provisions.

Where experienced MT technicians lose marks

  • Current type questions. Mixing up which current gives the deepest penetration, which gives the best mobility, and which is hardest to demagnetise is the most common error we see.
  • Field direction logic. Circular fields detect longitudinal discontinuities. Longitudinal fields detect transverse ones. Under exam pressure candidates reverse it.
  • Coil maths. L/D ratio, fill factor and ampere-turns appear as calculation items. Bench technicians often use a posted technique chart and never compute them.
  • Indicator limits. A pie gauge shows field direction, not adequate strength. Candidates who treat indicators as proof of adequacy lose marks.
  • Lighting numbers and conditions. UV-A intensity, ambient light limits and warm-up time for lamps are simple facts that are easy to forget.
  • Non-relevant indications. Magnetic writing, changes of section, permeability boundaries and heat-affected zones. The exam tests whether you can tell them from real discontinuities and explain why.

What an MT Level III actually does, and why the exam asks what it asks

The exam content makes more sense once you look at the job. An MT Level III in an inspection company or a manufacturing plant is responsible for decisions that a Level II follows but rarely makes.

Technique development. For a new part number or a new weld configuration, the Level III decides the magnetising technique, the current type, the amperage or ampere-turns, the shot sequence needed for coverage in two directions, and whether the continuous or residual method applies. That is why the exam spends so much time on current behaviour, coil relationships and field direction.

Procedure approval. The Level III writes or approves the procedure that fixes those choices, cites the governing standard, sets particle and lighting requirements, and defines acceptance criteria. When a client's auditor asks why a yoke is acceptable for a given thickness, or why a head shot was chosen over a central conductor, it is the Level III who answers.

Equipment and material control. A Level III sets up and audits the checks that keep a programme honest: ammeter accuracy checks on stationary units, yoke dead-weight lift tests, timer and quick-break checks, bath concentration by settling test, bath contamination checks, and UV-A and ambient light measurements. Exam questions on these checks are not trivia. They test whether you know what each check protects against.

Examinations and training. Under the employer's written practice, the Level III prepares, administers and grades the MT Level I and II general, specific and practical examinations, and signs off training records. Knowing the method well enough to write fair questions is part of the role.

Disposition of indications. When a Level II is unsure whether an indication is relevant, such as a sharp change of section, a heat-affected zone boundary or magnetic writing, the call comes up to the Level III, who has to explain it in writing.

Preparing with the job in mind makes the reading stick. Every topic in the plan above maps to one of these responsibilities.

ASNT Level III MT exam: frequently asked questions

How many questions are on the ASNT Level III MT exam?

90 multiple-choice questions in 2 hours, per ASNT's Level III examination listing.

What is the passing score?

70%.

Is the MT Level III exam easier than UT or RT?

It is shorter, with 90 questions in 2 hours against 135 in 4. It is not shallow. Magnetism theory, current behaviour and field design are tested at a depth most Level IIs have never needed.

Can I take MT as my first Level III method?

Yes. A first ASNT NDT Level III certificate needs the Basic plus at least one method exam, and MT qualifies.

How long should I study for MT Level III?

About 6 weeks at 6 to 8 hours a week after the Basic. Candidates adding MT to an existing Level III often need less.

Are there calculations on the MT exam?

Some. Coil L/D and ampere-turn relationships and amperage rules appear, along with questions that need you to reason about field strength and direction.

Does the exam cover both bench and yoke techniques?

Yes. Stationary bench units, portable yokes and prods, coils, central conductors and multidirectional systems are all within the body of knowledge.

How is an MT Level III certificate renewed?

Every five years, by 25 renewal points or by retaking the MT method exam.

Can I prepare for MT Level III online?

Yes. The exam is written and computer-based. Preparation runs live online, with the Practical NDT simulator available for method refresh.

Can MT and PT be prepared together?

Yes, and most candidates do. Ask for a combined MT and PT schedule.
