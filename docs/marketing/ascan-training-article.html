Browser-based A-scan practice in UT training: what it can and cannot do

Author: Anoop Rayavarapu, ASNT NDT Level III (UT, RT, MT, PT, VT), Atlantis NDT. Prepared for editorial submission; not yet submitted.

Most new ultrasonic testing (UT) trainees meet the A-scan for the first time on a flaw detector that is shared, booked and needed elsewhere. Their first sessions are spent on two things at once: learning to handle the probe and couplant, and learning to read the screen. The second skill does not need the instrument. It can be practised, at least in its basic form, on a simplified A-scan that runs in a web browser. This article describes what such an exercise is, where it can sit in an employer-based training programme, what it leaves out, and how trainees can use it without mistaking it for experience.

What a browser A-scan exercise is

The simplest useful model is a straight-beam (0°) pulse-echo probe on a flat plate. The plate contains one planar reflector parallel to the surface. The display plots amplitude, as a percentage of full-screen height, against sound path or time. The trainee moves the probe along the plate and changes a small number of settings: gain in decibels, range (how much sound path the screen shows), the material (which sets the velocity), the plate thickness, and a gate with a start, a width and a threshold.

Everything on the screen follows from one relationship, depth = velocity × time ÷ 2. In carbon steel with a longitudinal velocity of 5,920 m/s, a 25 mm plate puts the backwall echo at about 8.45 µs. A reflector at 12 mm returns an echo at about 4.05 µs. A trainee who can produce those numbers by hand, and then see them on the screen, has understood the time base.

Which reading skills it trains

A simplified display is enough to practise the first layer of A-scan interpretation:

  • Identifying signals. The initial pulse, the backwall echo, its repeats at exact multiples of the thickness, a reflector echo with its own repeat, and the drop in backwall amplitude when a reflector shadows the beam. Loss of backwall is worth teaching early, because it is evidence even when no reflector echo is obvious.
  • Gain and screen height. A change of 6 dB roughly doubles or halves echo height, because decibels are 20 × log10 of the amplitude ratio. An echo at 70% of screen height goes off the top of the screen with 6 dB more gain. Trainees learn that a saturated echo cannot be compared or sized.
  • Range. Too short a range pushes the backwall off the screen; too long a range crowds every echo into the left-hand side. Changing the material changes the time that a given range in millimetres represents.
  • Gates. A gate watches a window of the time base and reports the signal inside it. The useful lesson is that gain and gate threshold have to be read together. With a 40% threshold, an echo at about 72% of screen height trips the gate; reduce gain by 6 dB and the same echo, now near 36%, does not. In this particular model, reflector depth is a fraction of plate thickness. Increasing thickness therefore moves the modelled reflector and may require the gate to be moved; a real reflector does not move because an instrument setting changes.

None of this replaces calibration; it gives the trainee a vocabulary for the instrument sessions.

Where it fits in an SNT-TC-1A programme

Under ASNT Recommended Practice SNT-TC-1A, personnel certification is employer-based. The employer's written practice sets the training hours, the experience hours and the examinations, and the practical examination is taken on specimens with real equipment, where the candidate shows they can set up the instrument, follow the procedure, and record and evaluate results.

A browser exercise belongs on the training side of that picture, as a supplement to instruction, not on the experience side. Browser practice does not establish supervised industrial NDT experience. Keep it separate from experience records. The responsible Level III and employer should confirm whether a documented exercise forms part of the formal training programme under the applicable written practice. Record its purpose and limitations rather than representing it as work on real equipment.

Used that way, it is useful in three places:

  1. Before the first equipment session. Trainees arrive already knowing what a backwall echo, a repeat and a reflector look like, so instrument time goes to probe handling, couplant and calibration.
  2. In the classroom. An instructor can demonstrate gain, range and gating on a shared screen, then hand over to calibrated equipment and a reference block.
  3. Before the practical examination. Candidates can rehearse the reading and gating decisions, while the examination itself stays on real specimens.

What it does not simulate

The limitations should be stated to trainees as plainly as the features:

  • No beam spread, near field or attenuation, so echo height does not fall with depth as it does on a real part. That is the reason distance-amplitude correction exists.
  • No dead zone or probe ring-down near the surface.
  • No couplant, surface condition or contact pressure.
  • No angle beams, skip distances, mode conversion or weld geometry.
  • No calibration: no reference block, no velocity or zero-offset calibration, no DAC or TCG.
  • An idealised reflector: flat and parallel to the surface, so it always returns a strong echo. Real cracks can be tilted, rough or small and return far less.
  • No instrument settings such as probe frequency, pulser energy, filtering, rectification or reject.

A trainee who has used only a simplified display will over-estimate how clean real signals are. Instructors should say so before the first exercise, not after it.

A short routine for trainees

An example fifteen-minute routine for practice between classroom sessions:

  1. Set a 25 mm carbon steel plate and a range of about 60 mm. Identify the initial pulse, the backwall and its repeat. Work out the backwall time by hand before reading it on the screen.
  2. Move over the reflector. Note the new echo, its repeat and the drop in backwall. Calculate the reflector depth from its time.
  3. Add 6 dB of gain and watch the reflector echo saturate. Return to a setting where it sits below full-screen height.
  4. Shorten the range until the backwall leaves the screen, then restore it.
  5. Place a gate around the reflector echo with a 40% threshold. Reduce gain by 6 dB and observe that the gate no longer trips.
  6. Change the thickness and the material, re-gate the reflector, and repeat the hand calculation.

The next step is the same sequence on a real flaw detector with a reference block, under an instructor, where calibration, couplant and probe handling come in.

Summary

A browser A-scan is a reading exercise, not a qualification route. It teaches the time base, gain, range and gating. It does not teach calibration, scanning technique or the judgement real signals require, and it does not establish supervised industrial experience. Within those limits, it lets trainees spend scarce instrument time on the skills that need the instrument.
